\begin{abstract}
Near the tip of a gold nanorod the decay rate of a quantum emitter depends strongly on the orientation of its
transition dipole. Part of this anisotropy is generic---any curved metal surface quenches a perpendicular dipole more
strongly than a parallel one---and part is caused by the longitudinal plasmon resonance. We separate the two for a
capped gold nanorod (\SI{60}{nm}$\times$\SI{20}{nm}) with boundary-element calculations that match the exact Mie
solution to \SI{1}{\percent}, an equal-volume sphere as a control and a plasmon-eigenmode decomposition. At a
\SI{5}{nm} gap the axial dipole reaches a Purcell factor of \num{1.6e3} and a radiative enhancement of 117, about
\SI{90}{\percent} of which is carried by the longitudinal dipolar mode. The non-resonant part has an axial-to-transverse
ratio of 2--8 set by the tip curvature; the resonance raises the ratio to 49 (total rate) and 329 (radiative rate),
with a maximum near \SI{10}{nm} that grows from 19 to 872 between aspect ratios 2 and 5. A closed-form single-mode
model, based on the dipolar mode of the spheroid with the same apex curvature, reproduces the resonant enhancement of
30 rod, sphere and spheroid configurations without adjustable parameters and traces the anisotropy to the figure of merit
$(1-\varepsilon')^2/\varepsilon''$ of the metal. Without adjustable parameters, the model reproduces the maximum single-molecule fluorescence
enhancements measured near gold nanorods in two independent experiments ($10^3$ and $10^4$). We derive design rules for single-photon sources---gap versus intrinsic
quantum yield, collection through a glass substrate (\SI{79}{\percent} at $\mathrm{NA}=1.45$), purity limits set by
gold photoluminescence---and show that single emitters remain weakly coupled, that the conclusions are robust to the
gold permittivity data, nonlocal response, substrate and tip shape, and that at cryogenic temperatures the antenna
raises photon indistinguishability above \SI{96}{\percent}.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Purcell effect; local density of optical states; gold nanorod; plasmon eigenmodes;
quenching; single-photon source; boundary element method.

\section{Introduction}

The spontaneous emission rate of a quantum emitter is not an intrinsic property but depends on its
electromagnetic environment~\cite{purcell,novotny}. Metallic nanoparticles supporting localized surface plasmon
resonances confine light to volumes far below the diffraction limit and can enhance emission rates by several
orders of magnitude~\cite{maier,vanhulst,bharadwaj2009}. This has made plasmonic nanoantennas a candidate
platform for bright, fast single-photon sources~\cite{koenderink,hoang,akselrod,senellart}, in which a large
rate enhancement shortens the excited-state lifetime and raises the achievable repetition rate, and a short
radiative lifetime relative to the dephasing time is a prerequisite for indistinguishable photons~\cite{aharonovich}.

The price of plasmonic confinement is absorption. At emitter--metal separations of a few nanometres, energy is
transferred to non-radiative channels in the metal, and the fluorescence is quenched rather than
enhanced~\cite{anger,kuhn,ford,carminati2006}. The total decay rate and the radiative rate therefore have to be
treated separately, and their ratio---the antenna quantum efficiency---depends sensitively on distance,
wavelength and the intrinsic quantum yield of the emitter~\cite{bharadwaj2007,khurgin,lu2020}.

The optical response of metal nanoparticles is governed by their size, shape and dielectric environment, which set
the frequency, width and near-field distribution of the localized resonances~\cite{kelly2003,maier}. For emitters
within a few nanometres of the metal, two further regimes become relevant and are the subject of quantum
plasmonics~\cite{tame2013}: when the emitter--plasmon coupling rate exceeds the plasmon and emitter losses, the
system enters strong coupling, which has been reached for single molecules only in sub-nanometre gaps of
particle-on-mirror cavities~\cite{chikkaraddy2016,torma2015}; and below about \SI{1}{nm} electron spill-out,
nonlocality and tunnelling modify the metal response itself~\cite{savage2012,zhu2016,yang2019}. Which of these
regimes applies sets the appropriate description of the emitted light: a weakly coupled emitter keeps its quantum
statistics and is described by modified decay rates, whereas strong coupling produces hybrid light--matter states.

Gold nanorods are attractive antennas because their longitudinal plasmon resonance is tunable through the
aspect ratio across the visible and near infrared~\cite{ni2008,sonnichsen}, they can be synthesised with good
control~\cite{nikoobakht}, and their tips concentrate the near field. Single-molecule experiments have
demonstrated fluorescence enhancements exceeding a thousandfold near individual gold nanorods~\cite{yuan,khatua},
down to emitters with quantum yields of $10^{-4}$~\cite{lu2020}. On the theory side, Mohammadi \emph{et al.}
computed radiative enhancements and antenna efficiencies for gold nanorods and nanospheroids as a function of
aspect ratio, volume and embedding medium~\cite{mohammadi,mohammadi2009}, and body-of-revolution solvers have
recently been extended to excitation and emission near core--shell rods~\cite{ugwuoke2026}. These studies, which
exploit the rotational symmetry of an emitter on the rod axis, consider the axial dipole and gaps of
\SI{10}{nm} or more.

A consequence of the nanorod's elongated shape is a strong dependence of the response on the orientation of the
emitter's transition dipole. Yet a dipole close to any metal surface---a sphere or a plane---also shows an
orientation dependence: in the quasi-static near field, energy transfer to the metal is twice as strong for a
dipole perpendicular to the surface as for a parallel one~\cite{ford}. It is therefore not obvious how much of the
anisotropy near a nanorod tip is generic and how much is caused by the longitudinal resonance that distinguishes
the nanorod from a compact particle. The distinction matters in practice: emitters such as colloidal quantum
dots, nanodiamonds or dye molecules are often randomly oriented, and orientation-averaged or scalar
descriptions---a single ``Purcell factor''---discard precisely the resonant part. A second, methodological issue
is that the total rate in a nanometre gap is difficult to converge with volume-discretisation methods, whereas the
radiative rate is not; conclusions about quenching then depend on the solver.

In this work we separate the two contributions in two independent ways and explain the result analytically. First, we compare the nanorod with an
equal-volume gold sphere, probed with axial (radial) and transverse (tangential) dipoles at the same gap. Second,
we expand the decay rate in the quasistatic plasmon eigenmodes of the particle~\cite{mayergoyz2005} and isolate
the contribution of the longitudinal dipolar mode from the non-resonant background. Third, we derive a closed-form
single-mode expression, based on the exact dipolar mode of a prolate spheroid, that reproduces the resonant
enhancement without adjustable parameters and identifies the material and geometric factors that control it. All rates are computed with a
boundary-element method validated against the exact Mie solution, and checked against two finite-difference
time-domain solvers. We then translate the results into design rules for plasmon-enhanced single-photon sources:
the dependence of the optimal gap on the emitter's intrinsic quantum yield, the tolerance to its position, the
effect of a substrate and of nonlocal response, and resonance-matched antennas for representative emitters.

\section{Theory}

\subsection{Decay rates and efficiency}
For a point dipole $\mathbf{p}=p\,\hat{\mathbf n}$ at $\mathbf r_0$ in the weak-coupling regime, the total decay
rate normalised to its value in the homogeneous host is~\cite{novotny}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Gamma_{\rm tot}}{\Gamma_0} = \frac{6\pi c}{\omega}\,
  \hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathrm{Im}\,\overleftrightarrow{\mathbf G}(\mathbf r_0,\mathbf r_0;\omega)\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}
  \equiv \Fp(\omega),
  \label{eq:ldos}
\end{equation}
where $\overleftrightarrow{\mathbf G}$ is the dyadic Green function. Classically, $\Fp=P_{\rm tot}/P_0$ is the
power delivered by a constant-amplitude dipole relative to the homogeneous host. The power radiated to the far
field, $T=P_{\rm rad}/P_0$, gives the radiative enhancement, and $\Fp-T$ is absorbed in the metal. For an emitter
of intrinsic quantum yield $q_0$, the quantum efficiency in the presence of the antenna is~\cite{bharadwaj2007}
\begin{equation}
  \eta(q_0) = \frac{T}{\Fp + (1-q_0)/q_0},
  \label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
which reduces to the antenna efficiency $\eta_a=T/\Fp$ for $q_0=1$. The total decay rate of the emitter is
multiplied by $q_0\Fp+1-q_0$.

For a dipole on the symmetry axis of a body of revolution the Green tensor is diagonal, with an axial component
and two equal transverse ones; the anisotropy is fully characterised by the ratios
$\Fp^{\parallel}/\Fp^{\perp}$ and $T^{\parallel}/T^{\perp}$. For a sphere, axial and transverse correspond to radial
and tangential dipoles, for which exact Mie expressions exist~\cite{ruppin,kim,mertens}. The equal-volume sphere
therefore provides a reference that has the same amount of gold and the same dipole--surface gap, but no
elongated resonance.

\subsection{Plasmon-eigenmode decomposition}
In the quasistatic limit the surface charge $\sigma$ induced on a particle obeys the boundary integral equation
$(\Lambda + F)\,\sigma = -\phi'_{\rm ext}$, where $F$ is the normal derivative of the Coulomb kernel on the surface,
$\phi'_{\rm ext}$ the normal derivative of the external (dipole) potential, and
$\Lambda(\omega)=2\pi\,(\varepsilon+\varepsilon_b)/(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_b)$ contains the permittivities of
the metal and the host~\cite{mayergoyz2005,hohenester2012}. Expanding in the right and left eigenvectors of $F$,
$F u_k=\lambda_k u_k$, $\tilde u_k F=\lambda_k\tilde u_k$, $\tilde u_k u_l=\delta_{kl}$, gives
\begin{equation}
  \sigma = -\sum_k \frac{u_k\,(\tilde u_k\cdot\phi'_{\rm ext})}{\Lambda(\omega)+\lambda_k},
  \qquad
  \Fp-1 = \sum_k \Delta\Fp^{(k)},
  \label{eq:modes}
\end{equation}
because the induced field at the dipole, and hence $\Fp-1$, is linear in $\sigma$. Each mode resonates where
$\mathrm{Re}\,\Lambda(\omega)=-\lambda_k$, and its weight is the product of its coupling to the dipole
($\tilde u_k\cdot\phi'_{\rm ext}$) and of the field it produces back at the dipole. For the rod, the longitudinal
dipolar mode is identified as the eigenvector with the largest axial oscillator strength. On the axis, it couples
only to the axial dipole, whereas the transverse dipole couples only to modes with azimuthal number $m=\pm1$. We
call $\Delta\Fp^{(L)}$ the resonant part and $\Fp-1-\Delta\Fp^{(L)}$ the background; the background contains the
high-order modes that accumulate near the surface-plasmon condition and describe the lossy-surface (quenching)
channel~\cite{ford,anger}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Geometry and material}
The nanorod is a gold cylinder of length \SI{40}{nm} and radius $R=\SI{10}{nm}$ terminated by two hemispherical
caps (total length $L=\SI{60}{nm}$, aspect ratio 3), a geometry accessible by seed-mediated
growth~\cite{nikoobakht}. The dipole is placed on the rod axis at a distance $d$ from the apex
($d=\SI{5}{nm}$ unless stated otherwise; \numrange{3}{30}{nm} in the gap study). The equal-volume sphere has
$R_{\rm sph}=\SI{15.874}{nm}$. The host is air unless stated; Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry} summarises the geometry and the dipole orientations. Gold is described by the tabulated
Johnson--Christy permittivity~\cite{johnson} in all boundary-element and Mie calculations.

\begin{figure}[tbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_geometry.pdf}
\caption{Geometry. (a) Capped gold nanorod ($L=\SI{60}{nm}$, $D=\SI{20}{nm}$) with an emitter on the axis at a gap $d$
from the apex, oriented along (axial, $z$) or perpendicular to (transverse, $x$) the rod axis. (b) Gold sphere of the
same volume, probed with radial and tangential dipoles at the same gap. (c) Emitter on a spacer shell of thickness $s$
around the tip, at polar angle $\theta$, with its dipole normal to the surface; variants with a glass substrate and in

\end{figure}

\subsection{Boundary-element method}
The rates are computed with the boundary-element method (BEM) of the MNPBEM17
toolbox~\cite{hohenester2012,waxenegger2015,hohenester2018}, which solves the full Maxwell equations for the
surface charges and currents and discretises only the particle boundary, with curved boundary elements. $\Fp$
follows from the induced field at the dipole and $T$ from the far field, both in units of the rate in the
homogeneous host. The rod surface is discretised with element sizes $h=3$, 2 and \SI{1.5}{nm} (720, 1550 and 2829
elements), the sphere with 400, 784 and 1444 vertices; all gaps of one geometry are solved simultaneously. The
eigenmode decomposition, Eq.~\eqref{eq:modes}, uses the 40 lowest quasistatic eigenmodes of the
\SI{2}{nm} mesh; the full quasistatic $\Fp$ is computed with the same mesh. Additional calculations, described
where they are used, include a glass substrate (layer Green function~\cite{waxenegger2015}), the hydrodynamic
nonlocal response through the equivalent local cover layer of Luo \emph{et al.}~\cite{luo2013}, plane-wave
excitation, other shapes and silver. All calculations ran on GitHub-hosted runners; the scripts are provided.

\subsection{Finite-difference time-domain cross-checks}
The rod and sphere were also computed with a commercial three-dimensional FDTD solver (Lumerical FDTD 2024~R1,
\SI{2}{nm} override mesh)~\cite{taflove,lumerical} and with a body-of-revolution FDTD in cylindrical coordinates
(Meep~\cite{meep}, grids of 2, 1.33 and \SI{1}{nm}), in which an axial dipole couples only to the $m=0$ field
harmonic. Details, the gold Drude--Lorentz fit required by the time-domain solvers and all benchmarks are given in
the Supplementary Information.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Validation and convergence}
For the equal-volume sphere at a \SI{5}{nm} gap the BEM reproduces the exact Mie solution for both dipole
orientations over \SIrange{500}{900}{nm} (Fig.~S3): with 1444 vertices the median deviation is
\SI{0.9}{\percent} for the radial $\Fp$ and \SI{1.4}{\percent} for the radial $T$, \SI{0.7}{\percent} and
\SI{1.0}{\percent} for the tangential dipole, and at most \SI{4}{\percent}; the deviation is of the same size at 10 and
\SI{20}{nm} and in water (Table~S1). For the rod (axial dipole, \SI{5}{nm} gap) the peak values change by
\SI{0.3}{\percent} ($\Fp$) and \SI{0.4}{\percent} ($T$) between the 2- and \SI{1.5}{nm} meshes, and the peak positions
not at all; a \SI{1}{nm} mesh (6324 elements, \SIrange{500}{900}{nm}) changes them by a further \SIrange{0.2}{0.3}{\percent} at 5 nm and by less than \SI{0.1}{\percent} at 10 and \SI{20}{nm}
(Table~S2, Fig.~S1). We quote the \SI{1.5}{nm} mesh. The three-dimensional FDTD reproduces the radiative peak
(119 at \SI{616}{nm}, BEM 117 at \SI{608}{nm}) but gives a \SI{9}{\percent} lower $\Fp$ (1496 versus 1638); the
body-of-revolution FDTD reproduces $T$ to \SI{7}{\percent} on a \SI{1}{nm} grid but not $\Fp$ in the \SI{5}{nm}
gap. Against the exact solution for the sphere its $\Fp$ error decreases only slowly with the grid spacing, from
\SI{215}{\percent} (\SI{2}{nm}) to 108 and \SI{93}{\percent} (1.33 and \SI{1}{nm}), with \SI{48}{\percent}
remaining after linear extrapolation to zero spacing, while the error in $T$ falls from 10 to \SI{4}{\percent}; in the
three-dimensional solver the peak $\Fp$ of the rod changes non-monotonically with the override mesh (2027, 1496 and
3752 for 3, 2 and \SI{1.5}{nm}), whereas the radiative peak stays at \SI{616\pm4}{nm} and the efficiency at it
changes by about two percentage points (9.3, 8.6 and \SI{7.0}{\percent}; Supplementary Information). Volume discretisation is therefore adequate for the radiated power and the emission
pattern, but the total rate---and hence the quenching---in a nanometre gap requires a method that represents the
metal surface exactly.

\subsection{Axial versus transverse dipole, rod versus sphere}
Figure~\ref{fig:spectra} shows $\Fp$, $T$ and $\eta_a$ at a \SI{5}{nm} gap. For the axial dipole at the rod tip
$\Fp$ peaks at \num{1638} at \SI{604}{nm} and $T$ at 117 at \SI{608}{nm}, where $\eta_a=\SI{7.3}{\percent}$: more
than \SI{92}{\percent} of the energy is absorbed. A second $\Fp$ maximum at \SI{512}{nm} (848) coincides with the
onset of interband absorption and is almost purely non-radiative ($T=2.0$). The transverse dipole at the same
position has $T<0.5$ at all wavelengths---it radiates less than in free space---and its $\Fp$ maximum (248 at
\SI{508}{nm}) is non-radiative.

The equal-volume sphere separates the generic from the resonant contribution (Fig.~\ref{fig:orientation}). Its
radial-to-tangential ratio of $\Fp$ lies between 2.75 and 2.97 over the whole band, in agreement with the exact
solution (2.76--2.97) and near the quasi-static flat-surface value of two, without resonant structure. The rod
shows a similar ratio (3.3--4.0) below \SI{530}{nm} but rises to 49 at the longitudinal resonance. For the
radiated power the sphere ratio is 11--53, varying slowly with wavelength, because a tangential dipole near a metal
surface radiates poorly; the rod ratio is 5--6 below \SI{530}{nm} and 329 at resonance. At \SI{608}{nm} the rod
exceeds the sphere by factors of 15 in $\Fp$ and 23 in $T$, whereas the maxima of the two particles differ by only
2.0 in $\Fp$: with the same amount of gold, the elongated particle does not provide a larger total rate but moves a
radiative resonance out of the interband region.

\subsection{Mode decomposition}
The eigenmode analysis makes this separation explicit (Fig.~\ref{fig:modes}). The lowest non-trivial mode of the
rod is the longitudinal dipole ($\lambda_k=-5.01$, quasistatic resonance at \SI{595}{nm}, \SI{13}{nm} to the blue of
the retarded result); the transverse dipolar modes lie at $\lambda_k\approx-1.71$, which requires $\varepsilon\approx-1.75$ and therefore falls into the strongly damped interband region below \SI{500}{nm}. At a
\SI{5}{nm} gap and the resonance, \SI{91}{\percent} of $\Fp-1$ is carried by the longitudinal mode alone. The
remaining background is a smooth function of wavelength and its axial value exceeds the transverse rate by a
factor of 3.1, close to the sphere's ratio: \emph{the geometric anisotropy of the rod is that of any curved metal
surface, and the excess is the contribution of a single resonant mode.}

\begin{figure}[tbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_modes.pdf}
\caption{Plasmon-eigenmode decomposition (quasistatic BEM). (a) $\Fp$ of the axial dipole at a \SI{5}{nm} gap,
the contribution of the longitudinal dipolar mode, the remaining background and the transverse $\Fp$. (b) Share of
$\Fp-1$ carried by the longitudinal dipolar mode at its resonance versus gap. (c) Axial/transverse ratio of $\Fp$
(solid) and of the background alone (dotted) versus gap.}\label{fig:modes}
\end{figure}

The two contributions depend differently on the gap (Fig.~\ref{fig:modes}b,c). The background decays as the
near-field of a dipole above a lossy surface, approximately as $d^{-3}$, whereas the coupling to the dipolar mode
decays on the length scale of the tip radius. The share of the dipolar mode therefore increases from
\SI{80}{\percent} at \SI{3}{nm} to \SI{96}{\percent} at \SI{10}{nm} and \SI{98}{\percent} at \SI{20}{nm}, and the
total anisotropy $\Fp^{\parallel}/\Fp^{\perp}$ is non-monotonic: in the full retarded calculation it is 19 at
\SI{3}{nm}, 49 at \SI{5}{nm}, 136 at \SI{10}{nm}, 128 at \SI{15}{nm} and 75 at \SI{20}{nm}. At small gaps the
quenching background, which both orientations share, dilutes the resonant contribution; at large gaps the
transverse rate approaches its free-space value while the coupling to the mode keeps decreasing. The same
decomposition for aspect ratios 2--5 and for the sphere (Fig.~\ref{fig:modes}b, Table~S3) shows that the
background ratio is a property of the curved metal surface rather than of the shape: it is 2.5--3.7 for all four
rods and 2.3--2.4 for the sphere at gaps of 3--\SI{10}{nm}. What changes with the shape is the weight of the
dipolar mode. For the sphere its resonance lies in the interband region and it carries only \SI{9}{\percent} of
$\Fp-1$ at \SI{5}{nm}; for the rods its share at \SI{5}{nm} rises from \SI{57}{\percent} (aspect ratio 2) to
\SI{91}{\percent}, \SI{98}{\percent} and \SI{99}{\percent} (aspect ratios 3, 4, 5), as the resonance moves away from
the interband edge and gains oscillator strength. In the retarded calculation the maximum anisotropy, always found
near \SI{10}{nm}, accordingly grows from 19 (aspect ratio 2) to 54, 136, 621 and 872 (aspect ratios 2.5, 3, 4,
5).

The decomposition is quasistatic, but its main conclusion carries over to the retarded problem. Fitting the full
BEM spectra with a single complex pole, the quasinormal-mode form of a modal contribution~\cite{sauvan,lalanne}, plus a
smooth background gives the share of the dipolar mode at its resonance directly: \SI{88}{\percent} for aspect ratio 3,
\SI{97}{\percent} for aspect ratio 4 and essentially \SI{100}{\percent} for aspect ratio 5 at a \SI{5}{nm} gap,
with linewidths of 133, 87 and \SI{85}{meV} (Table~S4). Applied to the quasistatic spectra, where the exact answer is
known, the same fit reproduces the mode share to within 1--2 percentage points for aspect ratios 4 and 5 and
underestimates it by about 13 points for aspect ratio 3, whose resonance is closer to the interband edge; for
aspect ratio 2 the fit cannot separate mode and background. The background itself is the small difference of two
large numbers and is therefore taken from the quasistatic analysis.

\subsection{Closed-form model of the resonant enhancement}
The dominance of a single mode suggests a closed-form description. For a prolate spheroid the quasistatic problem of a
dipole on the axis is separable in spheroidal coordinates~\cite{gersten1981}: the axial dipole couples only to the
$m=0$ harmonics $Q_n(\xi)P_n(\eta)$, the transverse one only to $m=1$, and the decay rate is a sum over $n$ of
analytic terms. We evaluated this multipole series with the radiation reaction and dynamic depolarisation of the
dipolar terms included self-consistently~\cite{kelly2003} (Supplementary Information). For the
\SI{60}{nm}$\times$\SI{20}{nm} spheroid it reproduces the BEM background to within \SIrange{2}{6}{\percent} (axial
$\Fp$ at \SI{500}{nm}: 875 versus 896 at a \SI{5}{nm} gap), the transverse rate to within 6 and \SI{14}{\percent}
(median) at 5 and \SI{10}{nm}, and the axial peak to within \SI{10}{\percent} in $\Fp$ and \SI{5}{nm} in wavelength (Fig.~\ref{fig:law}a).

Keeping only the dipolar term, the resonant enhancement of the axial dipole at a distance $d$ from the apex follows
from the polarisability of the longitudinal mode and the field that this mode produces on the axis:
\begin{equation}
  \Fp^{\rm res}\simeq\frac{3}{2k^3}\,\mathrm{Im}\,\alpha_L\,E_1(z_0)^2,\qquad
  T^{\rm res}\simeq\bigl[1+\mathrm{Im}\,\alpha_L\,E_1(z_0)\bigr]^2,
  \label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Im}\,\alpha_L=\Bigl[\frac{4\pi\varepsilon''}{V(1-\varepsilon')^2}+\frac{2k^3}{3}\Bigr]^{-1},\qquad
  E_1(z_0)=\frac{3}{f^3}\Bigl[\frac{\xi}{\xi^2-1}-\frac12\ln\frac{\xi+1}{\xi-1}\Bigr],\quad \xi=\frac{z_0}{f},
  \label{eq:law2}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon=\varepsilon'+i\varepsilon''$ is the metal permittivity at the resonance, $V$ the particle volume,
$k$ the wavenumber, $z_0=L/2+d$ and $f$ the focal distance of the spheroid. The first term of $\mathrm{Im}\,\alpha_L$ is
the peak of the quasistatic Lorentzian, $V(1-\varepsilon')^2/(4\pi\varepsilon'')$, which uses
$1/L_z=1-\varepsilon'$ at resonance and therefore contains no shape factor; the second is radiation damping.
$E_1$ is the exact on-axis field of the dipolar spheroidal mode per unit dipole moment; far from the particle it
reduces to the point-dipole field $2/z_0^3$, while near the apex it is enhanced by the lightning-rod effect. For a
rod we use the spheroid with the same half-length and the same apex radius of curvature, $b^2/a=D/2$, i.e.
$f=\tfrac12\sqrt{L(L-D)}$. Single-mode expressions of this type, in which the Purcell factor is written through the
mode field at the tip, were derived for prolate spheroids by Shahbazyan~\cite{shahbazyan2016,shahbazyan2018}, who
also noted that transverse dipoles are not enhanced; the new elements here are the mapping of capped rods onto the
spheroid with the same apex curvature, the use of the computed resonance in a radiation-damped polarisability, and a
quantitative test against full retarded calculations. Without any adjustable parameter, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:law}--\eqref{eq:law2} reproduce the BEM
values of $\Fp$ and $T$ at the resonance for 30 configurations---aspect ratios 2--5, diameters 15--\SI{30}{nm}, a
silver rod and the spheroid, at gaps of 5, 10 and \SI{20}{nm}---over three orders of magnitude: the ratio of the BEM value to the closed form is 0.7--1.4 for $\Fp$ at gaps of 5 and
\SI{10}{nm} and 0.6--1.2 for $T$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:law}b,c; Table~S10). The largest deviations occur for aspect ratio 2, whose resonance lies at the interband
edge, where the background neglected in Eq.~\eqref{eq:law} is comparable to the modal term.

Equation~\eqref{eq:law} makes the origin of the resonant anisotropy transparent. The transverse rate is
non-resonant at $\lambda_L$, so the anisotropy at the resonance is $\Fp^{\rm res}/\Fp^{\perp}$, and its
material dependence enters only through the factor $(1-\varepsilon')^2/\varepsilon''$, a figure of merit of the
metal at the resonance wavelength. For gold it grows from 29 at \SI{560}{nm} to 84 at \SI{608}{nm}, 260 at \SI{680}{nm} and 370 at
\SI{760}{nm}, which, together with the larger volume and the stronger lightning-rod field of longer rods, accounts
for the increase of the maximum anisotropy from 19 to 872 between aspect ratios 2 and 5; for silver at
\SI{505}{nm} it is 400, hence the anisotropy of 677 of the silver rod at the same size. Increasing the diameter at
fixed aspect ratio increases $V$ but lowers $E_1$, because the apex becomes blunter, and radiation damping caps
$\mathrm{Im}\,\alpha_L$ at $3/(2k^3)$; the resonant factor therefore decreases with diameter (70, 49, 39 and 34 for
$D=15$, 20, 25 and \SI{30}{nm} at \SI{5}{nm}), while the radiative enhancement increases. The geometric factor, in
contrast, is determined by the background terms $n\geq2$, which depend only on the local surface curvature on the scale
of the gap.

\subsection{Gap dependence and the quantum-yield design rule}
Increasing the gap from 3 to \SI{20}{nm} (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}) leaves the radiative peak at \SIrange{608}{612}{nm}
while $\Fp$ falls from 3782 (at the interband maximum, \SI{508}{nm}) to 78 and $T$ from 214 to 8.2. The antenna
efficiency at the radiative peak increases only from \SI{6.6}{\percent} to \SI{11.6}{\percent}, whereas its
spectral maximum, reached far to the red of the resonance, rises from \SI{12}{\percent} to \SI{60}{\percent}. The
exact solution for the sphere shows the same trade-off (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}, lower row).

For a single-photon source the relevant figure of merit depends on the operating regime. Under saturated
(continuous-wave or high-repetition) excitation the photon rate scales with the radiative rate, i.e.\ with $T$,
for any $q_0$. Under pulsed excitation with a period much longer than the excited-state lifetime the number of
photons per pulse scales with $\eta(q_0)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:eta}~\cite{koenderink,hoang}. Figure~\ref{fig:q0} and
Table~\ref{tab:q0} show that the optimal gap depends on $q_0$. For $q_0=1$ the \SI{20}{nm} gap is best
(\SI{11.6}{\percent}) and the \SI{3}{nm} gap worst (\SI{6.6}{\percent}). For $q_0=0.01$ the order is reversed
beyond \SI{7}{nm}: gaps of 7 and \SI{10}{nm} raise the efficiency from \SI{1}{\percent} to \SI{7.4}{\percent} and
\SI{7.1}{\percent}, whereas \SI{20}{nm} gives \SI{4.8}{\percent}. A large $\Fp$ is needed to outcompete the
intrinsic non-radiative rate $(1-q_0)/q_0$, but beyond that point additional $\Fp$ only feeds absorption, so the
optimal gap increases with $q_0$.

\subsection{Excitation}
Fluorescence below saturation is also proportional to the excitation rate, i.e.\ to the local intensity
$|\mathbf E\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}|^2/|\mathbf E_0|^2$ at the emitter. For plane-wave illumination polarised along the
rod axis the axial intensity enhancement at a \SI{5}{nm} gap peaks at 119 at \SI{610}{nm}
(Fig.~S2), equal within \SI{2}{\percent} to the radiative enhancement $T=117$ at \SI{608}{nm}, as expected from
reciprocity for a dipolar antenna~\cite{novotny,bharadwaj2009}. At common pump wavelengths it is small: 3.2 at
\SI{405}{nm}, 3.0 at \SI{450}{nm} and 1.8 at \SI{532}{nm}, compared with 3.9, 3.9 and 6.7 for the sphere, whose
resonance lies near \SI{530}{nm}. Unless the emitter is pumped near the longitudinal resonance---for example through
an upper level of a molecule or quasi-resonantly for a quantum dot---the rod accelerates and redirects emission but
contributes little to excitation; for emitters pumped far above the gap the excitation enhancement is set by the
interband response and is no larger than for a compact particle.

\subsection{Robustness: emitter position, substrate and nonlocal response}
\emph{Emitter position.} Emitters are often attached to a dielectric spacer shell of fixed thickness rather than
placed exactly on the axis. We therefore moved the dipole over a shell of thickness $s$ around the hemispherical cap,
from the axis ($\theta=0$) to the start of the cylindrical side ($\theta=\SI{90}{\degree}$, a lateral offset of
$R+s$), with the dipole normal ($n$) or tangential ($t$) to the surface (Fig.~S4). For $s=\SI{5}{nm}$
the radiative enhancement of the normal dipole decreases only gradually, to \SI{68}{\percent} of its on-axis value at
$\theta=\SI{90}{\degree}$ (\SI{70}{\percent} for $s=\SI{10}{nm}$), and its efficiency from \SI{7.6}{\percent} to
\SI{6.8}{\percent}, because the longitudinal mode carries charge over the whole cap. Tangential dipoles remain dark
everywhere ($T\leq1.2$). For a spacer-shell geometry the relevant tolerance is therefore the orientation of the
transition dipole relative to the local surface normal, not the position on the cap.

\emph{Substrate.} In experiments the rod usually lies on a glass cover slip. We placed the rod with its axis
parallel to a glass half-space ($n=1.52$) at a height of \SI{1}{nm} (a ligand layer) and the dipole beyond the tip,
on the rod axis and parallel to the interface. The longitudinal resonance shifts from 608 to \SI{635}{nm}; at a
\SI{5}{nm} gap the radiated power rises to 266 and $\Fp$ to 2198 (both in units of the vacuum rate), and the antenna
efficiency increases from \SI{7.3}{\percent} to \SI{12.1}{\percent} (\SI{14.3}{\percent} and \SI{17.6}{\percent} at
10 and \SI{20}{nm}). Relative to the same dipole on bare glass, whose rate is 1.27 times the vacuum rate, the
enhancements are 209 ($T$) and \num{1.7e3} ($\Fp$). The substrate therefore does not weaken the conclusions; it
improves the efficiency, because the resonance moves further from the interband edge, and most of the emission
is directed into the glass (next subsection). The dipoles perpendicular to the rod axis remain dark ($T<2.3$).

\emph{Permittivity of gold.} Tabulated permittivities of gold differ by tens of percent in $\varepsilon''$. Repeating
the rod calculation with the data of Palik~\cite{palik}, Olmon \emph{et al.}~\cite{olmon2012} and McPeak
\emph{et al.}~\cite{mcpeak2015} instead of Johnson--Christy changes the radiative enhancement at a \SI{5}{nm} gap by
$+23$ to $+\SI{65}{\percent}$ and $\Fp$ by $-1$ to $+\SI{53}{\percent}$; adding surface (Kreibig) damping,
$A\hbar v_F/L_{\rm eff}$ with $A=0.33$ and $L_{\rm eff}=4V/S=\SI{17.8}{nm}$, lowers both by 13--\SI{14}{\percent}. The
evaporated-film data shift the resonance by at most \SI{16}{nm}; the coarser Palik data shift it by \SI{44}{nm}. None of
these changes the conclusions: the antenna efficiency at the radiative peak stays between 7.2 and \SI{9.1}{\percent}
(\SI{7.3}{\percent} with Johnson--Christy), and the maximum anisotropy at \SI{5}{nm} lies between 42 and 80 (49), with
the same gap dependence (Table~S11). The larger values for the newer data sets follow from their smaller
$\varepsilon''$, as expected from Eq.~\eqref{eq:law}.

\emph{Nonlocal response.} The hydrodynamic correction, estimated with the equivalent cover layer of Luo
\emph{et al.}~\cite{luo2013,raza2015,mortensen}, blue-shifts the radiative peak by \SI{5}{nm} and lowers $\Fp$ and $T$
at resonance by \SIrange{12}{17}{\percent} at gaps of 5 and \SI{10}{nm}; the antenna efficiency changes by less than
0.4 percentage points and the anisotropy increases slightly (from 49 to 70 at \SI{5}{nm}), because the transverse,
surface-dominated channel is suppressed more strongly. At \SI{3}{nm} the non-radiative interband maximum of $\Fp$ is
reduced by \SI{27}{\percent}. These changes are of the order expected for the cover-layer model with the tabulated
gold permittivity and do not affect the conclusions, but they indicate that absolute values of $\Fp$ at gaps of
\SI{3}{nm} and below carry an additional model uncertainty of this size.

\subsection{Generality: aspect ratio, environment, size, shape and metal}
Figure~\ref{fig:aspect} shows the radiative enhancement and the antenna efficiency at the radiative peak for aspect
ratios 2--5 ($D=\SI{20}{nm}$) and for the aspect-ratio-3 rod in water. As the resonance moves from \SI{560}{nm}
to \SI{760}{nm}, $T$ at a \SI{5}{nm} gap rises from 18 to \num{1.0e3} and $\eta_a$ from \SI{2.5}{\percent} to
\SI{22.5}{\percent}, because the resonance leaves the interband region; in water the same rod resonates at
\SI{715}{nm} with $T=457$ and $\eta_a=\SI{19.8}{\percent}$. These trends agree with those found by Mohammadi
\emph{et al.} at a \SI{10}{nm} distance~\cite{mohammadi}; the efficiency at the radiative peak depends mainly on the
resonance wavelength and only weakly on the gap between 5 and \SI{20}{nm}. At fixed aspect ratio 3, the rod volume
matters: for diameters of 15, 20 and \SI{30}{nm} the efficiency at the radiative peak (\SI{5}{nm} gap) is
\SI{3.3}{\percent}, \SI{7.3}{\percent} and \SI{23.7}{\percent}, reflecting the growth of radiative damping with
volume, while the resonance shifts only from 605 to \SI{625}{nm} (\SI{14.0}{\percent} at \SI{615}{nm} for
$D=\SI{25}{nm}$). A prolate spheroid with the same length and diameter resonates at \SI{600}{nm} with $T=106$ and
$\eta_a=\SI{5.3}{\percent}$; its sharper apex (radius of curvature \SI{3.3}{nm}) raises the off-resonance ratio to
7.7, which shows that the geometric factor is set by the curvature of the surface on the scale of the gap. Real
nanorods often have flatter ends than hemispheres. Replacing the hemispherical caps of the aspect-ratio-3 rod by
half-spheroids with an axial semi-axis of 0.75, 0.5 and 0.35 times the radius (same length; Table~S5) lowers the
background ratio at a \SI{5}{nm} gap from 3.1 to 2.6, 2.1 and 1.8, towards the flat-surface value of two, while the
maximum anisotropy, 133--135 at \SI{10}{nm} for all four shapes, is unchanged; the flatter ends red-shift the
resonance by up to \SI{15}{nm} and raise $T$ from 118 to 157 and $\eta_a$ from \SI{7.6}{\percent} to
\SI{9.5}{\percent}. The geometric factor thus ranges from about 2 for flat ends to about 8 for sharp spheroidal tips,
whereas the resonant factor is a robust property of the longitudinal mode. A silver rod of the same
dimensions resonates at \SI{505}{nm} with $T=\num{1.1e3}$ and $\eta_a=\SI{38}{\percent}$, and its anisotropy reaches
677 in $\Fp$. For all gold rods the non-resonant ratio, read at \SI{500}{nm}, is 2.9--3.8 at a \SI{5}{nm} gap, so the
separation into a geometric factor of about three and a resonant factor that grows with the oscillator strength
of the longitudinal mode holds generally.

\subsection{Collection efficiency}
The far field of the BEM solution, sampled on 1444 directions, gives the fraction of the radiated power that enters
the cone of a collection objective. For the axial dipole at the radiative peak and a rod standing along the optical
axis, this fraction is \SI{1.2}{\percent} for $\mathrm{NA}=0.5$ and \SI{18.1}{\percent} for $\mathrm{NA}=0.9$
(\SIrange{15.8}{18.1}{\percent} for gaps of 5--\SI{20}{nm}), in agreement with the body-of-revolution FDTD
(\SI{18.0}{\percent}) and within \SI{7}{\percent} of a bare axial dipole (\SI{1.3}{\percent} and \SI{19.4}{\percent}):
the antenna multiplies the radiated power by two orders of magnitude but does not redirect it, in contrast to
multi-element antennas designed for directivity~\cite{taminiau2008}. If the same rod lies in the focal plane, the
objective collects \SI{9.4}{\percent} at $\mathrm{NA}=0.5$ and \SI{32.5}{\percent} at $\mathrm{NA}=0.9$, the value of
a dipole perpendicular to the optical axis (\SI{32.6}{\percent}). A rod standing along the optical axis therefore
wastes almost half of its emission, and the orientation of the antenna relative to the collection optics is as
important a design parameter as the gap.

On a glass substrate the substrate itself provides the remedy. We obtained the angular emission of the dipole next to
the rod on glass by reciprocity: the power emitted into a direction $\hat{\mathbf s}$ of medium $j$ is
$\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}\Omega\propto n_j\sum_{\rm pol}|\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E_{\rm loc}|^2$, where
$\mathbf E_{\rm loc}$ is the field at the emitter produced by a unit-amplitude plane wave incident from
$\hat{\mathbf s}$~\cite{novotny}, computed with the BEM for 1728 directions from both half-spaces. The method
reproduces the analytic result for a bare dipole above glass (radiative rate 1.298 versus 1.299 times the vacuum
rate, \SI{82.6}{\percent} versus \SI{82.7}{\percent} into the glass) and, for a lossless dielectric rod, gives a
radiated power equal to the independently computed total decay rate to within \SIrange{1}{7}{\percent} (Supplementary
Information). For the gold rod lying on glass, the axial dipole at a \SI{5}{nm} gap radiates $T=272$ at
\SI{640}{nm} (\SI{2}{\percent} from the value of the substrate calculation above), and \SI{82.7}{\percent} of this
power enters the glass, the same fraction as for a bare horizontal dipole at the same height: on glass, too, the
antenna enhances but does not redirect the emission. An oil-immersion objective below the substrate therefore
collects \SI{79}{\percent} ($\mathrm{NA}=1.45$) or \SI{65}{\percent} ($\mathrm{NA}=1.3$) of the radiated power,
whereas a dry objective above the sample ($\mathrm{NA}=0.9$) collects \SI{15}{\percent}. Collecting through the
substrate is thus four times more efficient than collecting from a rod standing along the optical axis, and the
gap does not change these fractions (\SIrange{5}{20}{nm}).

\subsection{Comparison with single-molecule experiments}
The same method can be compared directly with the single-molecule experiments of Khatua \emph{et al.}~\cite{khatua},
who measured the fluorescence enhancement of crystal violet ($q_0=0.0228$, emission maximum \SI{640}{nm}, lifetime
\SI{1.0}{ns}) diffusing in glycerol near individual gold nanorods of diameter \SI{25}{nm} on glass, for longitudinal
resonances between 560 and \SI{720}{nm} and excitation at 594 and \SI{633}{nm}. We modelled their rods (diameter
\SI{25}{nm}, lengths 39--\SI{70}{nm}) in a homogeneous medium of index 1.47, which represents glycerol on glass, with
the dipole on the axis and parallel to it, as in their analysis. The fluorescence enhancement below saturation is
$\xi=E_{\rm exc}E_{\rm em}$, with $E_{\rm exc}=|E_z|^2/|E_0|^2$ at the laser wavelength and
$E_{\rm em}=\langle\eta(q_0)\rangle/q_0$ averaged over the emission band (Fig.~\ref{fig:khatua}). For the
\SI{47}{nm} rod, whose computed resonance (\SI{625}{nm}) is closest to the optimum, we obtain $E_{\rm exc}=127$ and
$E_{\rm em}=7.1$ at a \SI{5}{nm} gap, hence $\xi\approx900$, and $\xi\approx\num{1.3e3}$ at \SI{3}{nm}; Khatua
\emph{et al.} estimated $E_{\rm exc}\approx130$ and $E_{\rm em}\approx9$ with a discrete-dipole model. Figure~\ref{fig:khatua}
compares the calculation with the maximum enhancements they measured on 11 individual rods (digitised from their
Fig.~5). Near the optimum the agreement is good: for the four rods with resonances between 615 and \SI{650}{nm} under
\SI{633}{nm} excitation the measured maxima, 400--\num{1450}, lie within about a factor of two of the values calculated for
gaps of 3--\SI{5}{nm}, and the position of the optimum (resonance 625--\SI{650}{nm}) is reproduced. Away from the
optimum the measured enhancements decrease more slowly than calculated: under \SI{594}{nm} excitation rods with
resonances at 640--\SI{670}{nm} still give 94--208, three to seven times the value at a \SI{5}{nm} gap, and the same
underestimate is present in the authors' own calculations. Because the excitation enhancement scales with the overlap
of the laser with the plasmon line, a broader effective resonance---from size and shape dispersion, surface damping,
the glass substrate and molecules at off-axis positions, which see the transverse and interband response---flattens the
dependence on the resonance wavelength without changing the maximum. We tested two of these candidates directly
(Supplementary Information): surface (Kreibig) damping lowers the calculated maxima by about \SI{25}{\percent} but
does not flatten the dependence, and placing the rods on glass beneath glycerol, with excitation through the
substrate as in the experiment, changes the enhancement by at most \SI{6}{\percent}. Neither accounts for the
broader measured dependence, which therefore points to molecules away from the axis and to the dispersion of rod
shapes, rather than to the homogeneous response of an ideal rod. The computed total decay rate is shortened by
factors of 11--27 for the best rods, consistent with the measured lifetimes of 100--\SI{450}{ps} (unenhanced:
\SI{1.0}{ns}), which were limited by the instrument response of about \SI{120}{ps}.

A second, independent test is provided by Lu \emph{et al.}~\cite{lu2020}, who detected single molecules of a dye with
$q_0=\num{1.3e-4}$ (emission maximum about \SI{730}{nm}) near gold nanorods of diameter \SI{25}{nm} in toluene
($n=1.496$) under \SI{671}{nm} excitation. In this regime the emission enhancement is close to its upper limit
$T/\Fp\cdot q_0^{-1}$ and probes the radiative rate rather than the quantum efficiency. The same calculation (seven rod
lengths, gaps of 1.5--\SI{5}{nm}) gives a maximum enhancement of \num{9.7e4} for a resonance at 670--\SI{680}{nm} and a
\SI{1.5}{nm} gap, comparable to their own estimate of \num{5e4}; at the resonance of their best rod (\SI{667}{nm}) it
gives \num{1.6e4} at a \SI{5}{nm} gap and \num{7.9e4} at \SI{1.5}{nm}, compared with the measured \num{6.5e3} under
circular polarisation, corresponding to \num{1.3e4} for polarisation along the rod. For a rod resonating at
\SI{638}{nm} the measured value drops by a factor of 3.3; the calculated drop, a factor of 7.6, is again steeper. Both
experiments thus show the same pattern: the model, without adjustable parameters, reproduces the magnitude and the
position of the optimum, and it overestimates the selectivity with respect to the resonance wavelength, as expected
from inhomogeneous broadening in single-particle experiments. Because $q_0$ is small in both experiments, they probe
the regime in which the antenna improves the efficiency itself; the near-unity-yield regime remains to be tested
experimentally.

\subsection{Quantum regime: coupling strength, indistinguishability and photon statistics}
The rate-based description used so far assumes weak coupling. For a single plasmon mode of energy width
$\hbar\kappa$ in the bad-cavity limit, the mode-induced decay rate is $\Gamma_{\rm mode}=4g^2/\kappa$, so that the
emitter--plasmon coupling follows from the modal Purcell factor as
$\hbar g=\tfrac12\sqrt{\Fp^{\rm mode}\,\hbar\Gamma_0\,\hbar\kappa}$, and strong coupling requires
$g>(\kappa+\gamma)/4$~\cite{torma2015}, i.e.\ $\Fp^{\rm mode}>\kappa/(4\Gamma_0)$ for a spectrally narrow emitter.
Taking $\Fp^{\rm mode}$ and $\kappa$ from the retarded pole fits and a radiative lifetime of \SI{1}{ns}---an upper
bound on $\Gamma_0$ for dyes, quantum dots and colour centres---we find $\hbar g=\SI{5.3}{meV}$ at a \SI{5}{nm} gap
for aspect ratio 3 ($\hbar\kappa=\SI{133}{meV}$), \SI{16}{\percent} of the threshold, and at most \SI{37}{\percent}
(\SI{7.9}{meV}, aspect ratio 5) for any rod and gap studied (Table~\ref{tab:quantum}). Strong coupling would require
modal Purcell factors of \numrange{3e4}{5e4}, one to two orders of magnitude above the present ones, consistent with
the sub-nanometre gaps needed experimentally~\cite{chikkaraddy2016}; room-temperature emitter linewidths of tens of
meV raise the threshold further. All emitters considered here are therefore weakly coupled, which justifies the rate
description and implies that the antenna preserves the single-photon statistics of a single emitter. The main
threat to antibunching is instead the photoluminescence of the gold itself, which is excited by the same laser and
emitted in the same band, at the plasmon resonance. Its quantum yield is only of order
$10^{-6}$~\cite{yorulmaz2012}, but the absorption cross-section of the rod (BEM: \SI{1.6e3}{nm^2} at
\SI{405}{nm}, \num{1.5e3} at \SI{532}{nm} and \SI{9.5e3}{nm^2} at the resonance) exceeds that of a molecule by five
orders of magnitude. For pulsed excitation that excites the emitter with probability $1-1/e$, the ratio of background
to signal photons is $B/S=\sigma_{\rm abs}Y_{\rm PL}/[(1-e^{-1})\,\sigma_eE_{\rm exc}\,\eta]$, and the Poissonian
background raises $g^{(2)}(0)$ to $(2SB+B^2)/(S+B)^2$. For an emitter with an absorption cross-section
$\sigma_e=\SI{e-16}{cm^2}$, typical of dye molecules and colour centres, the rod at a \SI{5}{nm} gap gives
$g^{(2)}(0)=0.77$ under \SI{405}{nm} and 0.87 under \SI{532}{nm} excitation, i.e.\ the source is no longer a
single-photon source; resonant excitation at the plasmon reduces this to 0.28. For quantum dots or perovskite
nanocrystals ($\sigma_e\approx\SI{e-14}{cm^2}$) $g^{(2)}(0)$ stays between 0.003 and 0.07 for all gaps and pump wavelengths.
Purity therefore sets a further design rule: $g^{(2)}(0)<0.1$ requires $\sigma_e\gtrsim$ 2--\SI{4e-15}{cm^2} for
non-resonant and $\gtrsim\SI{4e-16}{cm^2}$ for resonant excitation at a \SI{5}{nm} gap (Table~S12), with spectral or
temporal filtering of the gold emission as the remedy for weak absorbers. These estimates assume that all gold
emission falls into the detection band and are therefore upper bounds on $g^{(2)}(0)$.

The Purcell effect does change a quantum property of the emitted light, its indistinguishability. For an emitter with
pure dephasing rate $\gamma^*$ in the bad-cavity regime, $I=\Gamma/(\Gamma+2\gamma^*)$ with $\Gamma=\Fp\Gamma_0$
($q_0=1$)~\cite{grange2015}. At room temperature ($\hbar\gamma^*\approx\SI{10}{meV}$) $I$ stays below
\SI{13}{\percent} even for $\Fp=\num{4.5e3}$, so plasmonic nanorods cannot make dephased room-temperature emitters
emit indistinguishable photons. At cryogenic temperatures ($\hbar\gamma^*=1$--\SI{10}{\micro eV}), however, the same
antenna raises $I$ from 3--\SI{25}{\percent} for the bare emitter to above \SI{96}{\percent} for $\Fp\geq80$
(\SI{20}{nm} gap) and above \SI{98}{\percent} at the \SI{5}{nm} gap, at the cost of the antenna efficiency of
Fig.~\ref{fig:q0}. The room-temperature permittivity used here is conservative for these estimates: removing the phonon
contribution from the Drude damping of gold (\SI{0.061}{eV} to \SI{0.039}{eV}, or to \SI{0.020}{eV} as a lower bound)
leaves the resonance unchanged and raises $\Fp$ by 10--\SI{21}{\percent}, $T$ by 26--\SI{57}{\percent} and the antenna
efficiency from 7.3 to 8.3--\SI{9.5}{\percent} at a \SI{5}{nm} gap, so that $I$ changes by less than 0.5 percentage points for
$\hbar\gamma^*=\SI{1}{\micro eV}$ and by less than three for \SI{10}{\micro eV} (Table~S11). For a cryogenic source of indistinguishable photons the trade-off is therefore between $I$, which
favours small gaps, and the efficiency, which favours large ones.

\section{Discussion}
The orientation dependence of emission near a nanorod tip consists of two factors with different origins. A
non-resonant factor describes how strongly the curved metal surface quenches a perpendicular relative to a parallel
dipole; it is about three for hemispherical tips and the equal-volume sphere, decreases towards the flat-surface
value of two for flatter ends and grows for sharper tips, but does not depend on the plasmon resonance. On top of it,
a single mode---the longitudinal dipolar plasmon---contributes a resonant factor that grows with the oscillator
strength of that mode, from 19 to 872 between aspect ratios 2 and 5, and is insensitive to the tip shape. The
equal-volume control and the eigenmode decomposition lead independently to this separation, and the decomposition
explains why the anisotropy peaks at an intermediate gap: the background is shared by both orientations and
dominates at small gaps, whereas the coupling to the mode decays more slowly.

For randomly oriented emitters on the axis, $\langle\Fp\rangle=(\Fp^{\parallel}+2\Fp^{\perp})/3=555$ and
$\langle T\rangle=39$ at \SI{608}{nm} and a \SI{5}{nm} gap, three times below the axial values, while the averaged
efficiency (\SI{7.0}{\percent}) is close to the axial one. The ensemble is bimodal: the transverse two thirds of the
orientations are quenched ($\eta_a=\SI{1.1}{\percent}$, $T=0.35$). An enhancement factor measured on an orientation
ensemble~\cite{yuan,khatua} therefore hides a population of nearly dark emitters, which matters when each emitter's
orientation is fixed.

For single-photon sources the results give simple rules: a gap of 5--\SI{10}{nm} for poor emitters, for which the
antenna raises the efficiency, and \SI{15}{nm} or more for near-unity emitters, for which it mainly accelerates the
emission; an emission wavelength well to the red of the interband edge (above about \SI{650}{nm} for gold), which
favours longer and thicker rods, flatter ends and a glass substrate; resonant excitation if an excitation enhancement
is wanted, and an emitter with a large absorption cross-section, or spectral filtering, to keep the gold
photoluminescence below the single-photon signal; and a rod lying in the focal plane, ideally on glass and collected through the substrate with an
oil-immersion objective. Methodologically, volume-discretisation
solvers reproduce the radiated power, the resonance and the emission pattern, but statements about quenching and the
optimal gap require a surface method or careful convergence studies.

The limitations of the present study are the local-response description of gold beyond the hydrodynamic estimate,
which is adequate at gaps of a few nanometres but not below about \SI{1}{nm}~\cite{xu,ciraci,zhu2016}; surface
scattering~\cite{kreibig,coronado}; the point-dipole, weak-coupling description of the emitter, which
Table~\ref{tab:quantum} justifies; and the quasistatic eigenmode analysis, which shifts the resonance by \SI{13}{nm}
and gives anisotropy ratios about \SI{35}{\percent} lower than the retarded ones while reproducing their gap
dependence. The retarded pole fits confirm the dominance of the dipolar mode but do not resolve the background; a full
quasinormal-mode expansion~\cite{sauvan,lalanne} would remove this limitation.

\section{Conclusion}
Using converged boundary-element calculations, an equal-volume sphere as a control and a plasmon-eigenmode
decomposition, we have separated the orientational anisotropy of the Purcell enhancement near a gold nanorod tip
into a geometric factor set by the curvature of the metal surface (about three for hemispherical tips and the
equal-volume sphere) and a resonant factor carried by the
longitudinal dipolar mode, which reaches 49 in the total and 329 in the radiative rate for an aspect-ratio-3 rod at a
\SI{5}{nm} gap, peaks near a \SI{10}{nm} gap and grows with the aspect ratio. A closed-form single-mode model
reproduces the resonant enhancement of 30 configurations without adjustable parameters and shows that it scales with the
volume, the lightning-rod field of the apex and the figure of merit $(1-\varepsilon')^2/\varepsilon''$ of the metal
at the resonance. More than \SI{92}{\percent} of the
emitted power is absorbed at the Purcell maximum for a \SI{5}{nm} gap; the optimal gap for a single-photon source
depends on the intrinsic quantum yield of the emitter, and resonance-matched rods emitting above \SI{650}{nm}, on
glass and in the focal plane of the collection optics, offer the best compromise between speed, brightness and
efficiency. The model reproduces, without adjustable parameters, the magnitude and the optimum of the
single-molecule fluorescence enhancement measured in two independent experiments, while the measured dependence on
the resonance wavelength is broader, as expected from inhomogeneous broadening. Simulation scripts, raw spectra and analysis code are openly available.
